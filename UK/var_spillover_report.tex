\section*{Methodology: VAR on Nelson-Siegel Factors}

We decompose the UK, US, and Euro-area government yield curves into three latent factors via the Nelson-Siegel model:
\begin{equation*}
    y(\tau) = \underbrace{\beta_1}_{\text{Level}} + \underbrace{\beta_2 \cdot \frac{1-e^{-\lambda\tau}}{\lambda\tau}}_{\text{Slope}} + \underbrace{\beta_3 \left(\frac{1-e^{-\lambda\tau}}{\lambda\tau} - e^{-\lambda\tau}\right)}_{\text{Curvature}}
\end{equation*}

\noindent where $\lambda=0.10$ (optimised by RMSE). Factors are extracted daily from 6 maturities (1Y--20Y) for each region, then resampled to weekly frequency. A 9-variable VAR(1) is estimated on:

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Cholesky ordering:} US$_{\text{Level}}$ $\rightarrow$ US$_{\text{Slope}}$ $\rightarrow$ US$_{\text{Curv}}$ $\rightarrow$ EU$_{\text{Level}}$ $\rightarrow$ EU$_{\text{Slope}}$ $\rightarrow$ EU$_{\text{Curv}}$ $\rightarrow$ UK$_{\text{Level}}$ $\rightarrow$ UK$_{\text{Slope}}$ $\rightarrow$ UK$_{\text{Curv}}$

\smallskip
\noindent Rationale: US rates are most exogenous (global reserve currency); UK is the ``receiver'' we focus on.

\begin{table}[H]

\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation:} US rates \textit{predictively lead} UK Level and Curvature with a 1-week lag. EU rates do \textit{not} Granger-cause UK factors---their influence is purely contemporaneous (same-day co-movement captured in the residual correlation structure).

\section*{Shock Propagation: How Foreign Rate Shocks Hit the UK Curve}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_irf.png}
    \vspace{-0.3cm}
    \caption{\small Orthogonalised IRFs: each panel shows the response of a UK factor (row) to a 1-s.d. shock in a foreign factor (column) over 40 weeks. Blue shading = 95\% bootstrap CI.}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Reading the IRF grid:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item \textbf{US Level $\rightarrow$ UK Level:} A shock to long-run US rates raises UK long rates by $\sim$0.15 s.d. on impact, persisting for 30+ weeks. This is the ``global real rate'' channel---Treasury sell-offs transmit directly to gilts.
    \item \textbf{US Curvature $\rightarrow$ UK Curvature:} The strongest single spillover. A US term-premium shock propagates immediately and almost 1-for-1 to the UK belly of the curve, reflecting shared risk appetite and carry positioning.
    \item \textbf{EU $\rightarrow$ UK:} Weaker individually but statistically significant on impact for Level and Curvature. EU shocks decay faster than US shocks (fully absorbed within 15 weeks).
    \item \textbf{Slope spillovers:} Relatively muted. Monetary policy stance (slope) remains largely idiosyncratic---BoE, Fed, and ECB diverge.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{table}[H]

\noindent\textbf{Key takeaway:} The UK curve is \textit{not} insulated from global moves. At the 1-year horizon, nearly half of the variation in UK term premia (curvature) is driven by US Treasuries. For trading, this means: large UST sell-offs (bear steepeners, duration supply shocks) mechanically propagate to gilts, particularly in the 5Y--10Y belly.

\section*{UK Gilt Market Context: May 2026}

\subsection*{Macro \& Fiscal Backdrop}

\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item \textbf{Inflation:} CPI at 3.3\% (March 2026), up from 3.0\% in February. Energy-driven (Iran war oil shock, Ofgem cap $\uparrow$20\% from July). BoE projects 3.0--3.5\% through Q3 2026.
    \item \textbf{Growth:} GDP grew 0.5\% in the three months to February 2026; OBR forecasts full-year 2026 growth at 1.1\% (down from 1.4\% in 2025).
    \item \textbf{BoE MPC (30 April):} Held at 3.75\%, 8--1 vote (1 member voted for a hike). Markets now price \textit{no cuts} in 2026, with $\sim$3 hikes priced.
    \item \textbf{Gilt issuance:} DMO remit for 2026--27 set at \pounds252.1bn (above consensus of \pounds245bn). Heavy tilt to short/medium maturities. Revised upward on 23 April 2026.
    \item \textbf{Debt:} PSND at 94.3\% of GDP, forecast to rise to 96.1\%. OBR flags ``fundamentally unsustainable'' trajectory without further consolidation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Recent Market Price Action}

\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item 10Y gilt yield: \textbf{5.06\%} (30 Apr), highest since July 2008.
    \item 30Y gilt yield: \textbf{5.74\%} (5 May), highest since 1998 --- up 13bp on the day.
    \item The sell-off accelerated after the MPC hawkish dissent and municipal election uncertainty.
    \item The April US--Iran ceasefire temporarily pulled yields 15--20bp lower; unwinding of ceasefire optimism is now reversing that move.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Key Drivers Ahead}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep, leftmargin=1.4em]
    \item \textbf{US Treasury moves remain the dominant risk factor.} Our VAR shows 30\%+ of UK Level variance is explained by USTs. Any further UST sell-off (e.g., on US fiscal concerns or Fed repricing) will mechanically widen gilt yields.
    \item \textbf{Energy prices / Iran situation.} A re-escalation would push UK CPI above 3.5\%, forcing BoE hikes and steepening the curve. Ceasefire durability is the swing factor.
    \item \textbf{Gilt supply.} The \pounds252bn remit is front-loaded with auctions. Poor bid-to-cover ratios would signal absorption stress, particularly in the 10--20Y sector where foreign spillover is highest.
    \item \textbf{BoE rate path.} The 8--1 split suggests a June hike is live. Hawkish repricing would flatten the slope (short-end up, long-end anchored by growth concerns).
    \item \textbf{Fiscal credibility.} NIESR flags gilt market ``derailment'' risk. Any revision to spending plans or OBR downgrades could trigger a repeat of the 2022 LDI episode in the long end.
\end{enumerate}
